# María Luisa Pacheco — Biography

## Chapter 1 — La Paz (1919–1951)
María Luisa Mariaca Dietrich was born on September 22, 1919 in La Paz [VERIFICAR: the Smithsonian indicates 1918]. She was the daughter of modernist architect and urbanist Julio Mariaca Pando [VERIFICAR: father's dates, 1890–1936] and [PENDIENTE: mother's name]. She learned her first drawing tools in her father's architectural studio. In 1936 she enrolled in the Academia Nacional de Bellas Artes Hernando Siles, studying under indigenist masters Cecilio Guzmán de Rojas and Jorge de la Reza, where she later taught as a professor. In the mid-1940s she also produced sculpture. From 1946 she worked at the newspaper La Razón as portraitist and illustrator, ultimately directing its arts and literary section. ## Chapter 2 — Madrid (1951–1952)
With a fellowship from the Spanish government, she studied at the Real Academia de Bellas Artes de San Fernando and in the studio of Cubist painter Daniel Vázquez Díaz. She analyzed the work of Picasso, Braque, and Juan Gris.

> "En cuanto llegué a Madrid fui al Museo del Prado. Al ver los cuadros de Velásquez y el Greco me sentí a punto de desplomarme."
> *(As soon as I arrived in Madrid I went to the Prado Museum. Seeing the paintings of Velázquez and El Greco, I felt on the verge of collapsing.)*

> "El arte tuvo un nuevo significado para mí."
> *(Art took on an entirely new meaning for me.)*

## Chapter 3 — Los Ocho Contemporáneos (1952–1956)
Back in La Paz, she co-founded the group Los Ocho Contemporáneos, also known as the Generation of '52. In 1953, the collective organized the first exhibition of abstract painting in Bolivia at the Salón Municipal in La Paz. That same year she won First Prize at the Salón Pedro Domingo Murillo [VERIFICAR: with the work *Siembra*]. Her painting during this stage brought together Cubist fragmentation and Andean, Quechua, and Aymara motifs.

## Chapter 4 — New York (1956–1982)
In 1956 she moved to New York at age 37 [VERIFICAR: arrived as a young mother with two children; one source states she separated that year]. Initially she worked as an illustrator for LIFE magazine and as a textile designer [VERIFICAR: at New Line Designers Studio]. She received three consecutive Guggenheim Fellowships (1958, 1959, and 1960) and the Painting Prize at the São Paulo Biennial in 1959. She was represented by the Lee Ault & Company gallery [VERIFICAR: the gallery opened in 1971 inspired by her work]. In New York her painting turned fully abstract, influenced by Abstract Expressionism and European Informalisme, and with ever-greater physical matter: oil applied with palette knife, sand, balsa wood, cardboard, and collage. She was never disconnected from Bolivia: she served as artistic director of the INBO Biennials in La Paz [VERIFICAR: 1975, 1977, and 1981] and delivered a lecture on abstract art at the Museo Nacional de Arte in 1976.

> "La pintura actual, antes de ser un paisaje o una figura, es esencialmente una superficie plana cubierta de colores."
> *(Contemporary painting, before being a landscape or a figure, is essentially a flat surface covered with colors.)*

> "Yo comienzo a pintar cuando se establece un contacto entre la obra y la imagen que formé en mi mente."
> *(I begin to paint when contact is forged between the physical work and the image formed in my mind.)*

## Chapter 5 — Legacy
She passed away in New York in April 1982 [VERIFICAR: April 21 or 23], at the age of 62. That same year her daughter, María Eugenia Ascarrunz, donated her papers to the Archives of American Art at the Smithsonian. Today she is recognized as the 20th-century Bolivian artist of greatest international reach and the trailblazer who introduced abstraction to her homeland. She influenced artists such as Alfredo La Placa and Enrique Arnal.

> "No debemos reemplazar el pasado, sólo tenemos que añadir un eslabón más."
> *(We must not replace the past; we only have to add one more link to the chain.)*
